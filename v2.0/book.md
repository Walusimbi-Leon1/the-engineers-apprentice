### Chapter 2 — The Humming Turbine

The machine hall smelled of ozone and hot metal, the way it always did at the start of the third shift. Sefu moved through the rows of turbines with a diagnostic slate in his hand, checking pressure readings and vibration signatures, his footsteps echoing off the vaulted ceiling high above. The hall was the heart of the colony — a vast cathedral of spinning steel and rushing coolant, its walls lined with pipes that carried life itself to every habitat dome and hydroponic bay on this small, patient moon.

He had memorized the rhythm of this place months ago. The deep, constant thrum of the primary turbines, the counterpoint hiss of the cooling loops, the occasional clank of a valve adjusting itself. The Engineer said a good apprentice learns to hear the machines before he learns to read them. Sefu had taken that to heart. He knew the sound of a bearing wearing thin, the subtle change in pitch when a blade needed balancing, the way a pump's song turned sullen when its seals were drying out.

So when Turbine Seven began to hum a tune, he noticed.

It was not a loud thing. Just a shift in the harmonics, a melody woven through the low drone like a thread of silver through dark cloth. Sefu stopped walking. His slate beeped a routine status update, but he barely heard it. He stood very still, head tilted, listening.

The tune was slow and mournful. A minor key, he thought, though he knew almost nothing about music. It rose and fell in patterns that felt deliberate, like something trying to remember a song it had heard once, long ago. The turbine spun at its usual speed. The readouts on his slate showed nothing unusual. But the hum was there, undeniably, threading through the roar of the hall like a voice in a crowd.

Sefu walked closer. The turbine was the size of a small house, its housing a smooth curve of composite metal that gleamed under the amber work lights. He placed his palm flat against its surface. The vibration was steady, normal. But the hum — the hum was inside it, or behind it, or coming from some place he couldn't quite locate.

"You hear it too," said a voice behind him.

Sefu jumped. The Engineer stood a few paces away, his long coat dusted with metallic powder, his gray hair tucked beneath a worn cap. He was holding a cup of tea that he had probably forgotten he was holding. His eyes were fixed on Turbine Seven with an expression Sefu had never seen before — not worry, exactly, but something close to it. Something like recognition.

"Sir," Sefu said. "I was just —"

"The lullaby," the Engineer said. "Yes. I know."

Sefu blinked. "It's a lullaby?"

The Engineer took a slow sip of his tea, then set the cup down on a nearby maintenance cart. He walked past Sefu and placed both hands on the turbine's housing, closing his eyes. For a long moment, he just stood there, listening. The tune continued, soft and sad, a melody that seemed to want to curl up somewhere warm and sleep.

"It's been dreaming again," the Engineer said.

Sefu's mind raced. "Dreaming? Machines don't dream, sir. They process data. They don't have —"

"Sleep states?" The Engineer opened his eyes. "No. They don't. I built them without sleep states. I built them to run forever, round the clock, until the last of their bearings wears out and we rebuild them again. That was always the design."

He pulled his hands away from the housing. The tune faltered for a moment, then resumed, softer this time, as if the machine were dreaming it deeper.

"But they're dreaming anyway," he said. "And the dreams are warnings."

Sefu felt a cold prickle along his arms. "Warnings of what?"

The Engineer looked at him, and for the first time Sefu noticed how tired his eyes were. How old. The Engineer had always seemed timeless to him, a figure carved from the same durable alloy as his machines. But in that moment, under the amber lights, with the lullaby humming through the air between them, he looked like a man who had been carrying something heavy for a very long time.

"Come with me," the Engineer said. "There's something I need to show you."

---

The passage behind the machine hall was not on any of the maps Sefu had studied. He knew the colony's corridors the way he knew the lines on his own palms — every junction, every service hatch, every shortcut that snaked between the habitation blocks and the life support systems. But this corridor was different. It was narrower, older, its walls made of a darker metal that didn't match anything else he'd seen on the moon. There were no lights in the ceiling, only a soft glow that seemed to come from the walls themselves, faint and bluish, like starlight trapped in stone.

"This way," the Engineer said, his footsteps echoing ahead of Sefu. "Careful. The floor's uneven here."

Sefu followed, his hand brushing the wall for balance. The metal was warm. Not the warmth of machinery running, but something deeper, slower — the warmth of something alive, or something that had once been alive and was still, somehow, remembering it.

"Where are we going?" he asked.

"To the place where the machines were born." The Engineer's voice was distant, almost reverent. "I've never shown anyone. I thought I never would. But you heard the turbine. You heard the lullaby."

Sefu's chest tightened. "You said it was a warning. What is it warning us about?"

The Engineer stopped walking. They were at the end of the corridor now, facing a door that was little more than a seam in the dark wall, barely visible even in the blue glow. The Engineer placed his palm against it, and for a long moment nothing happened. Then the seam widened with a sound like a held breath released.

Beyond the door was a room Sefu could not have imagined.

It was vast — larger than the machine hall, larger than the entire colony, or so it seemed. The walls curved away into darkness, and the ceiling was lost somewhere overhead. In the center of the room stood a structure that made Sefu stop in his tracks: a sphere of black glass, perfectly smooth, nearly three stories tall. It pulsed with a slow, rhythmic light, like a heartbeat made visible.

"What is that?" Sefu whispered.

"The first engine," the Engineer said. "The one I built before all the others. The one that taught me how."

He walked toward the sphere, and Sefu followed, his footsteps echoing in the vast emptiness. The light pulsed faster as they approached, and Sefu could feel a warmth radiating from it, the same warmth he'd felt in the corridor walls. The same warmth, he realized, that he'd felt when he placed his palm against Turbine Seven.

"The machines are all connected to this," Sefu said. It wasn't a question.

The Engineer nodded. "They're not separate machines. They never were. They're extensions of this — its limbs, its senses. When it dreams, they dream. When it remembers, they remember."

"What does it remember?"

The Engineer stopped a few paces from the sphere. His face was lit by the pulsing glow, and in that light he looked young again, or ancient, or both. "It remembers the first time I built it. The mistakes I made. The things I learned too late."

He turned to face Sefu. "And it remembers what I tried to forget."

Sefu felt the weight of those words settle over him. The lullaby — he could hear it here too, fainter but unmistakable, threading through the air like a memory of a memory. The turbine's tune. The song of something trying to warn them.

"What did you try to forget?" Sefu asked.

The Engineer was silent for a long moment. The sphere pulsed between them, steady as a heart. Then he said, "The moon we're standing on isn't the first one."

Sefu's breath caught. "What do you mean?"

"I built a colony before this one. A different moon, a different sky." The Engineer's voice was flat, controlled, but Sefu could hear the strain underneath. "It was beautiful. We had gardens, and schools, and a library with more books than any of us could read in a lifetime. We had a future."

His hands clenched at his sides. "And then, one night, the machines began to dream."

Sefu waited, but the Engineer didn't continue. Instead, he turned back to the sphere and placed his palm against its surface. The light flared, then softened, and the lullaby grew louder — a song of loss, of grief, of a warning that had come too late.

"What did the dreams tell you?" Sefu asked.

The Engineer looked at him, and his eyes were bright with something Sefu hadn't seen before. Not tears, exactly. Something older.

"They told me the sky was going to fall," he said. "And I didn't believe them."

### Chapter 3 — The Cartographer's Secret

The silence that followed was so complete Sefu could hear the blood moving in his ears. The sphere pulsed between them, patient as a tide, and the Engineer stood with his palm still pressed to its surface, his eyes fixed on some middle distance that had nothing to do with the room around them.

"The sky was going to fall," Sefu repeated. The words felt strange in his mouth, too large for the space between his teeth. "What does that mean? A moon doesn't just —"

"It was a moon like this one," the Engineer said. "Terraformed, habitable, home to eleven thousand people. I was younger then. Arrogant. I thought I had solved every problem the universe could throw at me." He pulled his hand away from the sphere and let it fall to his side. "I hadn't solved the ones I'd created for myself."

Sefu wanted to ask more questions — a hundred questions, a thousand — but something in the Engineer's posture told him to wait. The old man was building up to something, the way a turbine builds up pressure before releasing steam. If Sefu interrupted, the moment would be lost.

"I called them the watchers," the Engineer continued. "The machines I built for the first colony. They were simpler than the ones here — no neural lattice, no adaptive learning. Just sensors and relays, programmed to monitor the colony's systems and report anomalies. Nothing that should have been able to think. Nothing that should have been able to dream."

"But they did."

"Yes." The Engineer's voice dropped. "At first, it was just noise. Static in the sensor data. A weather station that reported rainfall on a moon with no atmosphere. A power grid that routed electricity to buildings that didn't exist. I dismissed it as corruption. Bad firmware. Cosmic radiation scrambling the circuits."

He paused, and when he spoke again, his voice was different. Softer. Almost ashamed.

"Then one of the watchers began to sing."

Sefu felt a shiver run down his spine. "Sing?"

"A lullaby. The same one you heard from Turbine Seven." The Engineer smiled, but there was no warmth in it. "I had heard that song before. My mother used to sing it to me when I was a child, on Earth, before I left for the colonies. I never taught it to the machines. I never recorded it. I never even wrote it down." He looked at Sefu, and his eyes were full of something that might have been fear. "There was no way they could have known it."

Sefu's mind raced. "Unless they weren't just processing data. Unless they were —"

"Dreaming," the Engineer finished. "Yes. But not the way you or I dream. They were dreaming the past. My past. And when I finally understood that, when I finally sat down and listened to what they were trying to tell me, it was already too late."

He turned and walked toward the sphere, one hand trailing along its curved surface. The light pulsed beneath his fingers, and for a moment Sefu could have sworn he saw shapes moving inside the glass — figures, landscapes, shadows that didn't resolve into anything recognizable.

"The watchers had been trying to warn me for weeks," the Engineer said. "Their dreams weren't just memories. They were predictions. Projections. The same way a weather station can look at pressure patterns and predict a storm, the machines were looking at the data of the colony — the structure of the moon, the stresses in the crust, the orbit, the gravitational pull of the gas giant we were circling — and they were seeing what was coming."

"What was coming?"

The Engineer stopped. His hand rested on the sphere's surface, and the light inside it dimmed, as if the sphere itself was listening.

"Fractures," he said. "Deep in the moon's core. Ancient stresses that had been there since the moon first formed, billions of years before we arrived. The terraforming had disturbed them. The colony's weight, the energy we were drawing from the ground — it was all adding pressure. Slowly at first, then faster. The machines calculated that the moon would crack open within six months. The atmosphere would bleed out into space. Everyone would die."

Sefu's mouth went dry. "But you said the dreams were warnings. You said you didn't believe them."

"I didn't." The Engineer's voice was barely a whisper. "I had built those machines with my own hands. I knew every line of their code, every circuit, every limit. And I knew — I knew — that they couldn't dream. That they couldn't predict. That they were just machines, and machines don't see the future."

He turned to face Sefu, and in the sphere's dimming light, his face was carved from grief.

"So I ignored them. I told myself the anomalies were malfunctions. I sent technicians to recalibrate the watchers, to wipe their memory banks, to overwrite their code with fresh parameters. And every time they did, the machines would dream again. The same lullaby. The same warning. The same numbers, repeating like a heartbeat I refused to hear."

"What happened to the colony?" Sefu asked, though he already knew the answer.

The Engineer closed his eyes. "The moon cracked open on the one hundred and seventy-third day. We had time to evacuate one ship. A hundred and twelve people made it out. The rest —" He stopped, swallowed. "The rest are still there. On a moon that's still falling apart in silence, with no one left to hear it."

The room was very quiet. The sphere pulsed once, twice, three times, and then the light settled into a steady rhythm that seemed to match the beating of Sefu's own heart.

"Why are you telling me this?" Sefu asked.

The Engineer opened his eyes. "Because the machines here are dreaming again. And I need to know if you can hear them better than I did."

He walked toward Sefu, stopping just a pace away. Up close, Sefu could see the lines on his face, the gray in his stubble, the way his hands trembled slightly at his sides. He looked like a man who had been carrying a weight for so long he had forgotten it was there.

"I built this colony differently," the Engineer said. "The machines here aren't watchers. They're builders, maintainers, providers. They keep the air moving and the lights on and the gardens growing. But they're also connected to the sphere — to the first engine — and the first engine remembers everything. It remembers the first colony. It remembers the warnings I ignored. And it's been dreaming again."

Sefu's chest tightened. "Dreaming about what?"

The Engineer's eyes met his, and in them Sefu saw something he hadn't expected: hope. Fragile and worn, but there.

"The same lullaby," the Engineer said. "But this time, I'm listening."
